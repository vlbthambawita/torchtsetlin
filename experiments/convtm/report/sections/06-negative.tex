\section{What did not work, and what we got wrong}

\subsection{Predictions that failed}
Both specialists pre-registered falsifiable predictions on the round's two hardest questions, and
\textbf{both predictions failed by their authors' own stated criteria}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{``Capacity saturates below 70\% by 8--16k clauses.''} Falsified by the published table
      the author then read for themselves: the curve gains +2.6 points per doubling at exactly the
      point saturation was predicted. Withdrawn.
\item \textbf{``A counting pool is worth 5--12 points.''} The supporting information-theoretic
      argument was recomputed from measured quantiles and found to be wrong by a factor of 5.7 --- the
      original figure was a ceiling under a uniform distribution quoted as an estimate. Revised to
      +0--1.5 points, at or below the seed band, and withdrawn as a standalone candidate.
\item \textbf{``The clause-size effect is an artefact of batched feedback.''} Falsified: the effect
      survives sequential feedback almost unchanged. The author's diagnosis of their own failure is
      worth recording --- the direction was right and the magnitude wrong by a factor of about 20.
\item \textbf{``Clause length is governed by the admission threshold $1/(1+s)$.''} Falsified: a
      tenfold change in $s$ moves clause length by 7.7\%. The companion prediction sharing the same
      constant, the equilibrium firing rate, was confirmed to within 11--13\%.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Two corrections to the falsification method itself}
Both came from the members they convicted.

\textbf{A falsifier stated over a quantity the intervention itself changes is not a falsifier.} Both
of the first round's pre-registrations were stated over a clause-match distribution that is the
equilibrium of a model trained \emph{under the very pooling rule the candidate replaces}.

\textbf{A pre-registration must name at least one rival explanation and the measurement that
separates it}, not only the falsifier of the preferred one. The prediction that the clause-size effect
was a batching artefact was correctly aimed by the first rule and still mis-aimed, because it named
only the mechanism its author had thought of and never registered the cheaper rival --- an untuned
specificity parameter --- which was the explanation that actually had to be excluded.

\subsection{Errors in the conduct of the work}
Recorded because a report that lists only what went right is not evidence of anything.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The team's own rulebook was wrong and cost a day.} It stated, on the strength of an
      earlier measurement at budget 8, that clause-size control ``does not bind''. It binds at every
      budget a published recipe uses. Three agents were steered away from what turned out to be the
      largest measured effect in the programme.
\item \textbf{A signal sat unread in the data.} The diagnostic showing 156-literal clauses was in
      every record from the first run and printed on every epoch line; it was logged as a curiosity.
      The engineer's own summary: \emph{being right in a JSON nobody acts on is the same as being
      wrong}. An escalation threshold was added.
\item \textbf{Three file edits silently did nothing} --- a string replacement that no-ops on a missed
      target while reporting success. Two were still broken when discovered. A success message that is
      not evidence of success recurred three times in different guises (that one, a queue log that
      cannot distinguish ``working'' from ``all jobs crashed'', and a fail-soft handler that would
      have written an invalid record after hours of training).
\item \textbf{A hyperparameter was inert in the baseline.} $T$ exceeded the achievable class sum, so
      the margin never annealed. Correcting it \emph{increased} the headline effect by 2 points.
\item \textbf{The orchestrator misdiagnosed a running queue as a crash} and had to retract it before
      it entered the audit log; and separately caused GPU contention that inflated one arm's timings
      by authorising a job against a stale idle check.
\item \textbf{A HOG encoder was wrong in every parameter that mattered} and was caught only when the
      correct specification arrived. It was recoverable because it had been registered as an explicit
      ambiguity, so the harness was already refusing to score it.
\end{itemize}
